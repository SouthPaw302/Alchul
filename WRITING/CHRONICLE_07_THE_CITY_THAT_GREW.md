# Chronicle 07 — The City That Grew

*The Surface Zenith — approximately 70 million years ago, a provisional narrative window. Fictional reconstruction through the Alchul literary frame. Names and cultural meanings are translated conventions.*

Through Alchul, we return to an ordinary morning in their world. Not to a catastrophe, a great discovery or a moment of revelation, but to a civilization enjoying the achievements of its ancestors.

For a little while, we leave the future alone.

---

The city had no founding day.

Some of its oldest passages had once been animal trails. The great eastern hall had grown around trees whose roots had been guided by generations of inhabitants, each generation inheriting a structure that was both younger and older than itself.

No living resident remembered the beginning.

Sen had spent his early years beside the river, but had never traveled beyond the northern terraces. That morning, Eru took him across the settlement toward the higher districts.

The city changed as they climbed.

Water descended through channels cut into the hillside, passing through gardens, storage grounds and shaded pools. Some channels carried drinking water; others fed cultivated organisms whose growth was useful to the inhabitants. Their engineers understood which systems could share water and which had to remain separate.

The buildings were not arranged in streets as we would recognize them. They followed the contours of the land, opening into communal spaces, branching into sheltered passageways and disappearing beneath vegetation.

From a distance, much of the settlement resembled a forest.

From within, it was unmistakably a city.

## The inheritance of knowledge

They passed a group of juveniles gathered around an elderly inhabitant.

The elder was teaching them to recognize the internal weaknesses of a piece of stone. Each learner examined it differently. One attended to its surface, another to the faint response produced when it was struck, while a third tried to predict where a fracture would travel.

The youngest became impatient and broke the piece prematurely.

The elder appeared amused.

Sen remembered being that young, when everything that resisted him seemed to be an invitation to apply greater force.

Their ancestors had been hunters. The instinct to pursue, anticipate movement and recognize weakness had never disappeared. It had found new expressions in craftsmanship, engineering, medicine and intellectual competition.

They had not abandoned their predatory nature to become civilized.

They had civilized it.

## The higher districts

Here, the air carried unfamiliar scents.

Families from other settlements had brought different fermented preparations, medicinal materials, ornaments and cultivated plants. Individuals displayed patterns of color and personal decoration that indicated affiliations, achievements and sometimes nothing more significant than individual taste.

Eru stopped beside a collection of woven objects. Their construction was unlike anything Sen had seen along the river.

The maker came from a settlement several days away, where the ground remained dry for much of the year. Their people considered the river inhabitants excessively dependent on water.

Sen found the suggestion absurd.

Eru found it interesting.

The two communities had developed different solutions to similar problems. They traded not only objects but techniques, seeds, observations and knowledge.

Their civilization was not a single homogeneous society. Its settlements differed in customs, beliefs, specialization and attitudes toward the unfamiliar.

There were friendships between communities, rivalries, political disagreements and memories of old violence.

Intelligence had not eliminated conflict. It had made possible more ways of resolving it—and more reasons for it to occur.

## The evening Harvest

As the light diminished, the communal grounds filled.

The Harvest was no longer simply a gathering around naturally fermented food. It had become something ancient, culturally rich and deeply personal.

Some came for companionship. Others for the preparations themselves. Some gathered to exchange ideas, while others sought the unusual experiences that occasionally accompanied particular states of perception.

Not everyone experienced them.

Not everyone believed the accounts of those who did.

Sen remained beside Eru as the rhythms of the gathering gradually softened. Beyond the terraces, the forest was alive with the movements and calls of creatures that had never known civilization.

Above them, the stars emerged.

They had begun keeping records of the sky long before either Sen or Eru was born. They understood its recurring patterns, although much of what lay beyond their world remained unknown to them.

They had no idea that their descendants would one day travel among those distant lights.

That evening, they were simply inhabitants of Earth.

And Earth was still young to them.
